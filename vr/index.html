Background

Virtual Reality (VR) has emerged as a rapidly growing technological frontier, captivating users with its promise of transporting them to alternate realms and immersive digital experiences. However, in this exciting digital landscape, a significant divide exists, creating a substantial gap between the virtual and the real world.

Users can be interrupted by phone calls and emails, or may simply be concerned about losing track of time. This disparity has the potential to hinder users from achieving a truly immersive experience. Our team intended to design a product that could bridge this gap between virtual and reality so the user can maintain an immersive experience.

User Goals

  • Keep awareness of the physical surroundings.
  • Stay immersed in the virtual environment as much as possible.
  • Know when another person enters or needs to enter their space.
  • Receive SMS or social media messages.
  • Keep track of time.

Solution

A virtual system-integrated application that alerts users according to the significance of an event or message. It includes basic interaction with messaging platforms and smart-home or IoT devices. Unique notification methods in VR allow users to get the information they need while staying immersed.

Design Ideation

During the early phases of our design process, we came across a study by Rufat Rzayev and Sven Mayer titled “Notification in VR: The Impact of Placement, Task, and Environment.” Their research examined notification-placement strategies within VR and their levels of intrusiveness. It became a valuable foundation for our conceptual design.

The research introduced four distinct notification methods: On-wall, On-body, Floating, and Heads-up. To balance immersion and necessary information, we conducted a second round of interviews to gauge the urgency and importance of different types of information. We used more intrusive notifications for urgent messages and less intrusive ones for lower-priority information.

Wrist Display

  • Less intrusive
  • Subtle
  • Allows the user to stay immersed

Heads-up Display

  • More intrusive
  • Easily noticeable
  • Used for urgent interruptions

Storyboard — Heads-up Display

For urgent matters such as a doorbell, the application pauses the current VR session and uses sound with a heads-up display. Users can look up to see the notification source and use the camera feed to find out who rang the doorbell.

Storyboard — Wrist Display

For less urgent notifications, an icon appears on the left wrist of the user's virtual avatar with a sound alarm. The user can pause the current session to read the full message or ignore it; ignored notifications fade after ten seconds.

Prototype

The landing page lets users configure notification sources and methods, and set or preset a timer.

The wrist display shows the message source; bringing the right hand toward the wrist expands the notification.

Rapid Usability Study

Landing Page Prototype

  • Give users a basic introduction.
  • Adjust wrist-display volume.
  • Add a new application to the notification list.
  • Move an application from the general list to the urgent list.
  • Record completion time and administer the System Usability Scale.

Immersive Prototype

  • Introduce the application and a background task.
  • Present either an SMS wrist notification or a door-camera heads-up notification.
  • Record time to notice each notification and any difficulty proceeding.
  • Administer the System Usability Scale.

Findings and Iterations

UX Issue #1

The wrist notification was too subtle to grab users' attention. More than half of participants took over 30 seconds to notice the message after the cue was triggered.

Solution

Add separate volume settings for heads-up and wrist notifications, add haptic feedback, and increase the wrist-notification alarm rate.

UX Issue #2

Moving and adding an app on the landing page was not intuitive. Users interpreted the add button as a transfer function rather than a way to add a new source.

Initial landing page

Landing page after adjustment

Solution

Replace the hamburger icon with a drag handle, add a tangible tab below each application, and use a shadow to strengthen the visual cue for dragging.

Reflection
