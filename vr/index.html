Bridge the gap between virtual and reality

Construct an information bridge that can allow users to interact and process events and items in the real world while keeping immersion in the virtual environment.

Background

Virtual Reality (VR) has emerged as a rapidly growing technological frontier, captivating users with its promise of transporting them to alternate realms and immersive digital experiences. However, in this exciting digital landscape, a significant divide exists, creating a substantial gap between the virtual and the real world.

Users can be interrupted by phone calls and emails, or may simply be concerned about losing track of time. This disparity has the potential to hinder users from achieving a truly immersive experience. Our team intended to design a product that could bridge this gap between virtual and reality so the user can maintain an immersive experience.

User Goals

  • Keep awareness of the physical surroundings.
  • Stay immersed in the virtual environment as much as possible.
  • Know when another person enters or needs to enter their space.
  • Receive SMS or social media messages.
  • Keep track of time.

Solution

A virtual system-integrated application that alerts users according to the significance of an event or message. It includes basic interaction with messaging platforms and smart-home or IoT devices. Unique notification methods in VR allow users to get the information they need while staying immersed.

Design Ideation

During the early phases of our design process, we came across a study by Rufat Rzayev and Sven Mayer titled “Notification in VR: The Impact of Placement, Task, and Environment.” Their research examined notification-placement strategies within VR and their levels of intrusiveness. It became a valuable foundation for our conceptual design.

The research introduced four distinct notification methods: On-wall, On-body, Floating, and Heads-up. To balance immersion and necessary information, we conducted a second round of interviews to gauge the urgency and importance of different types of information. We used more intrusive notifications for urgent messages and less intrusive ones for lower-priority information.

Storyboard --- Heads-up Display

For more urgent matters such as doorbells, it can be set to be notified through a heads-up display when friends or family ring your doorbell. The application will pause the current VR session along with a sound notification. Users can then look up to see the notification's source and use the camera feed to find out who rings their doorbell.

For less urgent notifications, it can be set to be notified through the wrist display where a notification icon will pop up on the left wrist of the user's virtual avatar along with a sound alarm. After the user perceives the notification, the user can choose to either pause the current VR session to read the more detailed message or ignore the notification.

Prototype

Landing page of the application in VR, where users can do the initial setup, customize the source of the notifications they will receive while in VR, as well as the type of notification method assigned to them. The user can also set and pre-set a timer on the landing page.

Wrist display example: Notifications on the wrist display show the source of the message. Holding the right hand close to the left wrist will expand the notification with brief message content.

Heads-up display example: front camera feed. If users connect their IoT camera, once the camera feed is set under the urgent list in the application settings, when the user's doorbell rings, the system will pause the VR session and display the corresponding camera feed.

Rapid Usability Study

After the initial prototype was implemented in VR, the team designed the following procedure to evaluate the initial prototypes:

  • Landing Page prototype
    • Users are given a basic introduction to the landing page.
    • Users are then given the following key tasks to complete:
      • Adjust the volume for the wrist display.
      • Add a new application to the notification list.
      • Change the corresponding application from the general notification list to the urgent list.
    • Time to complete each task is recorded. At the end of the session, the participant completes the System Usability Scale (SUS) survey.
  • Immersive prototype in VR
    • Users are given a basic introduction to the application.
    • A basic task (shoot the box) is implemented to mimic a general VR session's demand on the user.
    • Users are then given one of the following two scenarios at random:
      • Wrist notification from an SMS message.
      • Heads-up notification from the front-door camera feed.
    • Time for the user to notice each notification method is recorded. The researcher also records any difficulty proceeding.
    • At the end of the session, the participant completes the System Usability Scale (SUS) survey.

Findings and Iterations

UX Issue #1

The wrist notification was too subtle to grab users' attention. More than half of participants took over 30 seconds to notice the message after the cue was triggered.

Solution

Add separate volume settings for heads-up and wrist notifications, add haptic feedback, and increase the wrist-notification alarm rate.

UX Issue #2

Moving and adding an app on the landing page is not intuitive. Users attempted to use the add button on the urgent list to move apps from the general notification list to the urgent list. Many users interpreted the add button as a transfer function instead of adding a new application or source to the notification list.

Solution

The hamburger icon alone did not effectively convey the drag affordance for the tabs beneath the notification lists. To address this, the drag-handle icon was introduced as a replacement. We also implemented a tangible tab beneath each notification application, accompanied by a shadow to strengthen the visual cue for dragging.

Reflection

  • Design is an iterative process.
  • It can be challenging to eliminate biases during the data collection process, and we are instinctively oriented to put ourselves at the center of design rather than the users.
  • Diversity in a group is important. Team members from different backgrounds provide unique angles for designing the app and resolving the problem.
