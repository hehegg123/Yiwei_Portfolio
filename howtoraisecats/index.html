Overview

There were no existing mobile applications that taught beginning cat owners how to raise a cat in a structured way. It was difficult for potential cat keepers to take the first step. Our team was tasked with building an AR-based training application to teach users how to raise a cat.

The application features four key functions: interaction with a virtual cat, cat body-language review, hardware selection, and a cost estimator to prepare users to become successful cat owners.

Data Elicitation

The first step of my design process was user research. I recruited and interviewed Virginia Tech students who were interested in adopting a cat or had recently adopted one. Using quantitative and qualitative data from interviews and surveys, I defined two target-group profiles: Caroline, who was looking to adopt a cat, and Andy, who had recently adopted his first cat.

MVP Needs

List of minimum requirements identified from the research

  1. Adoption instruction: Walk through the adoption process and requirements.
  2. Clinical instruction: Explain regular clinical needs and signs that a cat needs care.
  3. Cat gesture changes: Show different gestures and their meanings.
  4. Hardware introduction: Explain the equipment needed for cat care.
  5. Interactive cat model: Teach different petting zones through reactions.
  6. Cost estimator: Provide an estimated cost that changes with users' choices.

Design

The main frustration we found was that cat-care knowledge is scattered across many sources, making it difficult to learn and comprehend as a whole. Some cat-care incidents also require a quick response, which is difficult if the owner must consciously recall unfamiliar knowledge.

Solution

Because the product aims to train users, we used knowledge transfer as the foundation of the design. Timon ten Berge and Rene van Hezewijk separate knowledge into two types: declarative knowledge and procedural knowledge.

Declarative Knowledge

Descriptive knowledge that can be encoded into long-term memory through repetition. Applying it requires retrieval and conscious processing, which can take time.

Procedural Knowledge

Knowledge of how to perform an activity. Once trained, it can become automated and require less conscious attention, allowing people to react more quickly.

The research showed that most existing cat-care resources rely on declarative knowledge. That provides a quick general understanding, but urgent cat-care situations may require owners to react before they can consciously retrieve information. The application therefore combines explanation with repeated, realistic interaction so important responses can become procedural knowledge.

Usability Testing

After completing the prototype, I recruited six participants from an HCI class and conducted three rounds of usability testing. During the first two rounds, participants received an introduction, completed tasks, and then shared feedback through an interview and System Usability Scale survey.

Usability Test Tasks

  1. Create an account and select a cat breed.
  2. Pet the cat in the main UI.
  3. Navigate to the adoption documents page.
  4. Select and place hardware.

First Iteration

  • Enlarged the Add button and reduced its transparency.
  • Added a gesture menu.
  • Added a text signifier shown when an icon is pressed.

Second Iteration

Participants wanted to choose the cat's age and name to build an emotional bond, and they were interested in learning how to choose food. The low intuitive score persisted, which led to a change in the third testing method.

  • Added age selection and naming after cat selection.
  • Added food-related documents accessible through the menu and main UI.

Third Iteration

To investigate the low intuitive score, I skipped the walkthrough and handed the prototype directly to participants. Users immediately tried to touch the virtual cat without selecting an interaction tool. Icons alone also failed to communicate their meaning.

  • Touching the virtual cat now triggers pet mode directly.
  • Added a tutorial after registration.

Future Plan & Takeaways

For future evaluation, I recommend recruiting at least 30 participants with no pet-care experience. Redesigns or new features should be considered only when they are reflected in more than five percent of the data.

  1. Provide a brief introduction to the prototype and testing process.
  2. Ask participants to complete predefined tasks without additional instruction.
  3. Provide a guided walkthrough of the prototype.
  4. Allow additional hands-on experience without further instruction.
  5. Ask participants to complete a SUS survey, cat-care quiz, and feedback survey.
User testing is key. Design is a continuous process of improving the experience, so it is important to listen to feedback.
Don't just ask—observe. Watching people use the application revealed insights that participants did not always verbalize.
The best design comes from collaboration. Different perspectives inspire ideas, while technical knowledge helps ground them in practical constraints.
